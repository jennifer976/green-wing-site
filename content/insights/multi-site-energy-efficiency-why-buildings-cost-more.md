# Same Business. Similar Buildings. Very Different Energy Bills. What's Going Wrong?

Two hotels from the same group. Similar room numbers, comparable facilities and broadly the same operating hours.

Yet one consistently consumes significantly more energy than the other.

The same situation can arise across restaurant chains, leisure centres, retail estates and other multi-site businesses.

It is tempting to attribute the difference to building age, occupancy or equipment. And sometimes those factors explain it.

But not always.

**The real question is whether the difference is justified—or whether one building is using energy it simply doesn't need.**

For businesses managing multiple properties, understanding that distinction can reveal opportunities to reduce operating costs without immediately investing in new equipment.

---

## Why Similar Buildings Don't Always Perform the Same

On paper, two properties can appear almost identical.

Both may have similar heating systems, refrigeration equipment, lighting and opening hours. They may even operate under the same maintenance contracts.

However, how those systems are used can differ considerably.

For example:

- **Heating schedules:** One building's heating starts two hours before staff arrive. Another starts four hours before.
- **Ventilation controls:** One location has properly configured ventilation controls. Another runs its ventilation continuously.
- **Refrigeration performance:** One site's refrigeration equipment is maintained and operating efficiently. Another has deteriorating door seals, dirty condensers or unsuitable settings.
- **Conflicting systems:** One building's heating and cooling systems operate independently. Another has periods when they work against each other.
- **Out-of-hours consumption:** One site actively manages energy use outside business hours. Another has never examined its overnight energy profile.

None of these differences necessarily appears on an electricity bill.

Yet repeated across every day of the year, they can contribute to a substantial difference in operating costs.

---

## The Problem With Comparing Energy Bills Alone

Many organisations monitor energy costs by site.

This provides a useful financial overview, but it does not necessarily reveal how efficiently each building operates.

A hotel with higher occupancy will naturally use more energy than one with fewer guests. A restaurant serving twice as many customers may have greater cooking, refrigeration and ventilation demand.

**Comparing total expenditure without considering these factors can lead to misleading conclusions.**

A more meaningful assessment looks at several measures together:

### 1. Energy Consumption

How many kilowatt-hours does the site use, rather than simply how much does it spend?

### 2. Operating Conditions

How do occupancy, trading hours, weather and production volumes influence consumption?

### 3. Equipment Performance

Are heating, cooling, refrigeration and electrical systems operating as intended?

### 4. Out-of-Hours Demand

How much energy is being consumed when the building is closed or operating at reduced capacity?

### 5. Controls and Maintenance

Are systems correctly configured, maintained and regularly reviewed?

By examining these factors, organisations can begin separating unavoidable operational demand from potentially unnecessary consumption.

## What Could a Small Improvement Mean Across an Entire Estate?

Consider a hypothetical hospitality group operating **40 locations**.

If a properly assessed and verified improvement reduced electricity consumption by 15,000 kWh per site annually, the estate-wide reduction would be:

**600,000 kWh of electricity per year.**

At an illustrative electricity cost of 22p per kWh, that equates to:

**£132,000 in annual avoided electricity expenditure.**

That is before considering any additional heating-related opportunities.

These figures are illustrative, not a forecast or guaranteed saving. The actual opportunity depends on each site's existing performance, energy tariffs, operational requirements and suitability for improvement.

**The important point is that relatively modest, repeatable improvements can become commercially significant when applied across multiple locations.**

---

## Why the Highest-Consuming Site Isn't Always the Best Place to Start

When reviewing an estate, it might seem logical to investigate the building with the largest energy bill first.

However, the highest-consuming site may already be operating efficiently relative to its size and activity.

Meanwhile, a smaller building could have avoidable consumption that is easier and less expensive to address.

This is why a useful energy improvement programme should consider more than total consumption.

It should establish:

1. **Where the strongest evidence of avoidable waste exists.**
2. **Which improvements can be achieved through operational adjustments or maintenance.**
3. **Which opportunities require additional monitoring or investment.**
4. **How quickly an intervention could deliver a measurable benefit.**
5. **Whether a successful improvement can be replicated across other sites.**

The objective is not necessarily to find the biggest project.

**It is to identify the most commercially sensible starting point.**

## From Individual Buildings to an Estate-Wide Improvement Programme

One of the advantages of managing multiple properties is the opportunity to learn from comparable sites.

An initial assessment may identify a control setting, operating practice or equipment issue worth investigating elsewhere.

However, repeating the same intervention without checking site conditions can be just as problematic as ignoring the opportunity altogether.

A change that works well in one building may not be suitable for another with different plant, occupancy or operating requirements.

A structured approach therefore involves:

- **Assessing the opportunity:** Understanding the building's current energy performance and identifying potential inefficiencies.
- **Establishing a baseline:** Recording consumption and relevant operating conditions before changes are made.
- **Implementing improvements:** Making suitable adjustments or introducing appropriate solutions.
- **Verifying the outcome:** Measuring performance after implementation to establish whether the expected improvement has been achieved.
- **Considering wider implementation:** Determining whether the results justify investigating similar opportunities across other locations.

This allows businesses to build an evidence-based programme rather than relying on generic savings assumptions.

It also provides finance and operations teams with clearer information when deciding where future investment should go.
